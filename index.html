Inside the cell

Cell part

The nuclear envelope

The nucleus is wrapped in two membranes instead of one. Together they are called the nuclear envelope (Clark et al., “Eukaryotic Cells”).

Structure
Two membranes, one inside the other.
Job
Keeps the DNA separate from everything else going on in the cell.

The outer membrane is actually connected to the rough ER (Clark et al., “Endomembrane System”). I think that is the coolest part, because it means the ER is right next to where the instructions come out.

Nuclear pores

The envelope has holes in it called nuclear pores. They are not just holes. They are gates that control what goes in and out of the nucleus (Clark et al., “Eukaryotic Cells”).

Structure
Gates that go through both membranes.
Job
Let mRNA and ribosome parts out, while the DNA stays inside.

Chromatin

The DNA inside the nucleus is wrapped around proteins. This is called chromatin (Clark et al., “Eukaryotic Cells”). If you stretched out all the DNA in one human cell, it would be about 2 meters long (Alberts et al.).

Structure
DNA wound around proteins, like thread on spools.
Job
Packs all that DNA into a tiny space. A gene can unwind when the cell needs to read it.

The nucleolus

The dark spot inside the nucleus is the nucleolus. It does not have a membrane around it. It is where ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins get put together into ribosome parts (Clark et al., “Eukaryotic Cells”).

Structure
A packed-together clump with no membrane.
Job
Builds the two halves of ribosomes. Then they leave through the nuclear pores.

Since there is no membrane, everything it needs is right there in one spot, so it can build ribosomes fast.

The rough ER

The rough ER is a bunch of flat, folded sacs. It looks “rough” because it is covered in ribosomes (Clark et al., “Endomembrane System”).

Structure
Folded sacs covered in ribosomes, with a space inside called the lumen.
Job
Makes proteins, folds them inside the lumen, then ships them to the Golgi in little bubbles called vesicles.

The folds give it a lot of surface area, so it fits a lot of ribosomes. Cells that send out a lot of protein, like liver cells, have tons of rough ER (Clark et al., “Endomembrane System”).

Ribosomes

Ribosomes are tiny protein builders made of rRNA and protein. Each one has two parts, a small one and a large one (Clark et al., “Eukaryotic Cells”).

Structure
Two parts that clamp around the mRNA.
Job
Read the mRNA three letters at a time (a codon) and link amino acids into a chain (Clark et al., “Ribosomes and Protein Synthesis”).

Ribosomes on the rough ER push the new chain right into the ER while they build it. You can try it yourself in Build a Protein.

Answering my question

So how does shape help?

Nucleus

Two membranes and gated pores keep the DNA safe but still let the instructions out.

Nucleolus

It has no membrane and is packed tight, so it can build ribosome parts fast.

Rough ER

Its folds fit lots of ribosomes, and it is connected to the nucleus, so the mRNA does not have far to go.

Ribosomes

Their two parts clamp the mRNA so they can read it in order and build the chain.

The limits of my model

My sketches are not to scale. Also, the protein in Build a Protein only has four amino acids. Real proteins usually have hundreds.
